Banks as Liquidity Providers

Why Do Banks Both Take Deposits AND Make Loans?

Kashyap, Rajan, and Stein (2002), Journal of Finance

The Puzzle

Traditional View: Banks do two separate things:

  1. Deposits (liabilities) → Provide safe, liquid claims
  2. Loans (assets) → Allocate capital, monitor borrowers

The Big Question:
Why does one institution need to do both?

Think about it…

  • Finance companies make loans without taking deposits
  • Money market funds take deposits without making loans
  • So why do banks combine them?

Why This Matters

Policy Implications:

  • Should we have “narrow banking”?
    • Deposits in safe mutual funds
    • Lending by separate finance companies
  • How does monetary policy work?
    • Bank lending channel requires deposits AND loans together

Core Issue

Is there a real synergy or just historical accident?

The Key Insight

Both deposits and loan commitments are the same product:

The Unifying Concept

The provision of liquidity on demand

  • Demand deposit = Put money in, take it out anytime
  • Loan commitment = Take money out anytime (up to a limit)

Once a commitment is extended:
It’s just a checking account with overdraft privileges!

The Economic Intuition

Problem: Providing liquidity on demand is costly

  • Must hold liquid assets (cash, securities) as buffer stock
  • Liquid assets are expensive:
    • Foregone interest
    • Double taxation
    • Agency costs (managers can misuse liquid assets)

Solution

Share the buffer stock costs across BOTH sides of balance sheet

How the Synergy Works

Imagine a bank needs $20 buffer for potential deposit withdrawals

Scenario 1: Just deposit-taking

  • Hold $20 in cash
  • Often sits idle when no withdrawals

Scenario 2: Deposits + Commitments

  • Same $20 buffer covers BOTH:
    • Deposit withdrawals (some days)
    • Commitment takedowns (other days)

The Key

As long as withdrawals and takedowns aren’t perfectly correlated,
the same buffer does double duty!

Historical Evidence

Medieval Banking (13th-14th century):

Money changers evolved from:

  1. Changing coins → Opening deposit accounts
  2. Deposits → Allowing overdrafts

Senator Robert Morris (1785):

“By becoming stockholders in a bank, the merchants had pooled their cash to make it go further… when one does not want his money, it is earning his share of the dividend from another”

Deposits and overdrafts were explicitly seen as drawing on common liquidity!

The Model

Model Setup: Timeline

Three dates: 0, 1, 2

  • Interest rate \(i\) (same both periods)
  • All payments at date 2 (no compounding)

Date 0:

  • Bank makes term loans \(L\)
  • Holds liquid assets \(S_0\)
  • Issues deposits \(D_0\)
  • Sells commitments \(C\)

Date 1:

  • Random deposit withdrawals
  • Random commitment takedowns
  • May need external finance

Assets

Term Loans \(L\):

  • Two-period rate: \(r(L)\) where \(r_L < 0\) (market power)
  • First-order condition: \[r + Lr_L = 2i\]

Liquid Assets \(S_0\):

  • Earn rate \(i\) but cost \(\tau\) per dollar held
  • Net return: \((i - \tau)S_0\)

Market Power: What does \(r_L < 0\) mean?

  • Bank faces downward-sloping loan demand: to lend more, must lower interest rate
  • \(r_L = dr/dL < 0\): marginal interest rate falls as loan volume increases
  • Assumption only limits scale of lending (avoids infinite loans); not central to synergy story

Why are liquid assets costly (\(\tau > 0\))?

  • \(\tau = i\) if held as non-interest-bearing cash
  • \(\tau > 0\) if securities (taxes, agency costs)

Liabilities

Deposits \(D_0\):

  • Pay zero interest (or below market)
  • Random fraction \(\omega\) withdrawn at date 1
    • \(\omega \in \{0, 1\}\) with equal probability
  • Exogenous “deposit franchise” value

External Finance \(e_0\), \(e_1\):

  • Date 0: Cost \(2i\) (frictionless)
  • Date 1: Cost \(ie_1 + \alpha e_1^2/2\) (adverse selection)
    • \(\alpha\) = degree of capital market imperfection

Commitments

Loan Commitments \(C\):

  • Revenue: \(fC\) where \(f_C > 0\), \(f_{CC} \leq 0\)
  • Random fraction \(z\) taken down at date 1
    • \(z \in \{0, 1\}\) with equal probability

Correlation Parameter

\(\rho\) = prob(\(\omega = 1 | z = 1\))

  • \(\rho = 1/2\): independent
  • \(\rho = 1\): perfectly positively correlated
  • \(\rho = 0\): perfectly negatively correlated

Budget Constraints

Date 0: \[L + S_0 = D_0 + e_0\]

Date 1: \[L + S_1 + zC = D_0(1-\omega) + e_0 + e_1\]

where \(S_1 \geq 0\) (can’t short liquid assets)

External Finance Need (Date 1)

\[e_1 = \max[zC + \omega D_0 - S_0, 0]\]

The Bank’s Problem

Maximize: \[E\{rL + fC + izC + (i-\tau)S_0 + iS_1 - 2ie_0 - ie_1 - \alpha e_1^2/2\}\]

Key Trade-offs:

  • More \(C\) → More commitment revenue BUT higher expected external finance costs
  • More \(S_0\) → Pay cost \(\tau\) NOW BUT save on expected external finance costs LATER

First-Order Conditions

Liquid Assets

\[\tau = -\frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{dE(e_1^2)}{dS_0}\]

Marginal cost of holding liquidity = Marginal benefit of reduced external finance

Commitments

\[f + Cf_C = \frac{\alpha}{2}\frac{dE(e_1^2)}{dC}\]

Marginal revenue from commitments = Marginal cost of increased external finance

Solving the Model: Four Regions

The solution depends on how \(S_0^*\) compares to \(C^*\) and \(D_0\):

Region Condition External Finance
1 \(S_0^* \leq \min(C^*, D_0)\) 3 out of 4 states
2 \(C^* \leq S_0^* \leq D_0\) Only if both shocks
3 \(D_0 \leq S_0^* \leq C^*\) Only if both shocks
4 \(S_0^* \geq \max(C^*, D_0)\) Fully self-insured

The Synergy (Region 1)

When \(S_0^* \leq \min(C^*, D_0)\):

\[S_0^* = \frac{C^* + D_0 - 2(\tau/\alpha)}{2-\rho}\]

\[f + C^*f_C = \frac{\alpha}{4-2\rho}[(2\rho - \rho^2 - 1)D_0 + (1-\rho)C^* + 2(\tau/\alpha)]\]

Taking derivative: \[\frac{dC^*}{dD_0} = \frac{\alpha(1-\rho)^2/(4-2\rho)}{d^2(fC)/dC^2 - \alpha(1-\rho)/(4-2\rho)} > 0\]

if \(\rho < 1\)

Intuition: Why More Deposits → More Commitments

Simple Example:

  • Bank has $100 deposits with 20% withdrawal risk
  • Needs $20 buffer for deposits alone

Now add $20 commitment business:

  • If commitment takedowns negatively correlated with deposit withdrawals:
    • Same $20 buffer covers both!
    • No additional liquid assets needed

More generally (any \(\rho < 1\))

  • More deposits → more buffer stock
  • Extra buffer can also cover commitment risk
  • Makes commitments cheaper to offer

When Does the Synergy Break Down?

Region 3: \(D_0 \leq S_0^* < C^*\)

\[f + C^*f_C = \tau\]

Commitment level independent of deposits!

Why?

  • Buffer stock already exceeds maximum deposit withdrawal
  • Deposits alone don’t stress liquidity position
  • Adding more deposits adds buffer ONLY for deposit+commitment scenarios
  • No marginal benefit for commitment capacity

A Numerical Example

Parameters: \(f(C) = 1 - 0.025C\), \(\rho = 0.5\), \(\alpha = 0.1\), \(\tau = 0.45\)

Main Predictions

Prediction 1 (Across institution types)

Banks will offer relatively more commitments than other lenders

Prediction 2 (Within banks - liquid assets)

\[\frac{\partial (\text{Liquid Assets})}{\partial (\text{Transactions Deposits})} > 0\]

Prediction 3 (Within banks - commitments)

\[\frac{\partial (\text{Commitments})}{\partial (\text{Transactions Deposits})} > 0\]

Joint interpretation (IV logic):
Commitments related to liquid assets driven by deposits

Beyond the Model: Additional Implications

Extension to Term Lending

Model focuses on commitments, but there’s a natural spillover to term loans

The Logic:

  1. Bank screens borrower for commitment (sunk cost)
  2. This information is valuable for term loans too
  3. Bank has advantage in offering term loans to same customer

Prediction Beyond the Core Model

Banks’ term lending should be disproportionately tilted toward customers who are heavy users of commitments

Example: Small firm with volatile working capital needs - Heavy user of credit lines → Bank learns about firm - Also more likely to get plant/equipment loans from banks (vs. finance companies)

Relation to Other Theories

Other explanations for deposits + lending:

  • Calomiris & Kahn (1991): Demandable deposits discipline bank managers
    • Sequential service → incentives to monitor
    • But why more for banks than industrial firms?
  • Diamond & Rajan (2001): Demandable claims let banks “create” liquidity
    • Can promise more than market value of assets

This Paper’s Distinctive Contribution

Diversification across BOTH sides of balance sheet

  • Others: Diversification across depositors OR across borrowers
  • Here: Deposits + Commitments share the same liquidity buffer
  • Focus on lines of credit (not just term loans) as key to synergy

Empirical Evidence

Data: Banks vs Other Lenders

National Survey of Small Business Finances (1993)

  • 4,637 small firms
  • All loans from each firm (by 1992)
  • Categorized by lender type AND loan type

Key Variables:

  • Unsecured lines of credit → Closest to model’s commitments
  • Total lines of credit → Broader measure
  • Survey question: “Where do you go for unexpected credit needs?”

Banks vs Finance Companies

Lines of Credit (% of lending):

Institution Type Unsecured Secured Total Lines
Banks/S&Ls 31% 39% 70%
Finance Companies 5% 45% 51%
Insurance Companies 11% 5% 16%

Key Finding

Banks dominate unsecured commitment lending!

  • 31% vs 5% for finance companies
  • 70% total commitment lending vs 51%

Unexpected Credit Needs

“Where do you first look for unexpected short-term credit?”

  • Over 70% said banks
  • Barely 1% said finance companies
  • <1% said insurance companies

Even relative to market share:

  • Finance companies: 11% of total lines
  • Insurance companies: 2% of total lines

Important

Banks are THE source for unexpected liquidity needs

Within-Bank Tests: Data

Call Reports (1992-1996)

  • All federally insured banks
  • Aggregate to holding company level
  • Time-average over 5 years

Why time-average?

  • Avoid mechanical correlation from high-frequency shocks
  • Example problem: Builder draws commitment AND withdraws deposits
    • Would create spurious positive correlation
  • 5-year average = target/steady-state values

Key Variables

DEPRAT: \(\dfrac{\text{Transactions deposits}}{\text{Total deposits}}\)

LIQRAT: \(\dfrac{\text{Cash + Securities + Fed Funds}}{\text{Assets}}\)

SECRAT: \(\dfrac{\text{Securities + Fed Funds}}{\text{Assets}}\)

COMRAT: \(\dfrac{\text{Commitments}}{\text{Commitments + Loans}}\)

Controls:

  • Log(Real Assets)
  • Loan composition
  • Regional dummies

Addressing Endogeneity

Concern

Maybe high loans → mechanically ↓ COMRAT and ↓ DEPRAT?

Solution: Look at C&I division separately

  • CICOMRAT = C&I commitments / (C&I commitments + C&I loans)
  • Restrict to banks where C&I = 10-50% of total loans

Logic (à la Lamont 1997)

  • Even if bank’s overall capital structure endogenous
  • Unlikely driven by one small division (C&I lending)

C&I Commitments vs Deposits

Dependent Variable: CICOMRAT (C&I commitments / C&I business)

Extensions: Three Follow-up Papers

Gatev & Strahan (2006): The Main Argument

Banks’ Advantage in Hedging Liquidity Risk

Core Finding:

Banks are a “safe haven” during market-wide liquidity shocks

When CP markets freeze:

  • CP spreads widen
  • Investors flee to safety
  • Deposit inflows to banks

Simultaneously:

  • Firms can’t roll CP
  • Draw down credit lines
  • Loan commitments activated

The Natural Hedge

The very event that triggers loan demand (commitment takedowns) also triggers deposit inflows

→ Banks receive cheap funding exactly when they need it most!

Gatev & Strahan (2006): Link to KRS

How this extends KRS (2002):

  1. Validates the correlation assumption:
    • KRS assumed \(\rho < 1\) (imperfect correlation)
    • Gatev & Strahan show \(\rho\) is actually negative during crises!
  2. Dynamic buffer replenishment:
    • KRS: Static buffer serves double duty
    • Gatev & Strahan: Buffer dynamically refills when most needed
  3. Reintermediation effect:
    • Modern deposit insurance → banks become liquidity sinks in crises
    • Unlike Great Depression (runs from banks to currency)

Tip

Implication: The synergy is even more powerful than KRS modeled

Gatev, Schuermann & Strahan (2009): The Main Argument

Managing Bank Liquidity Risk

Core Finding:

The deposit-commitment synergy is a risk-management hedge

Empirical Approach:

  • Look at stock return volatility (market-based risk measure)
  • Not just quantities (KRS approach), but prices

Key Results:

  • Unused commitments normally increase bank stock volatility (liquidity risk)
  • BUT: For banks with high transactions deposits, this increase is offset
  • Effect varies with market conditions:
    • Normal times: Low correlation (as KRS modeled)
    • Stress times: Strong negative correlation (hedge activates)

Gatev, Schuermann & Strahan (2009): Link to KRS

How this extends KRS (2002):

KRS (2002) approach:

  • Cross-sectional evidence
  • Balance sheet quantities
  • Static model

Finding: Banks with more deposits hold more commitments

GSS (2009) approach:

  • Time-series variation
  • Market prices (volatility)
  • State-dependent effects

Finding: Deposit-commitment synergy reduces systematic risk

Key Insight

Transactions deposits aren’t just a funding source—they’re a liquidity risk insurance policy for commitment lending

The “double duty” buffer in KRS actually shows up as lower risk in market prices

Acharya & Mora (2015): The Main Argument

A Crisis of Banks as Liquidity Providers

Core Finding:

During 2007-2009, the KRS synergy strained and nearly broke

The “Double Squeeze”:

  1. Asset side: Firms drew down credit lines heavily
    • Borrowers desperate for liquidity
    • Commitment takedowns surged
  2. Liability side: Deposit inflows weakened/reversed
    • Questions about bank solvency
    • Not a “safe haven” anymore
  3. Result: Banks became “liquidity seekers” instead of “liquidity providers”
    • Bid up deposit rates aggressively
    • Accessed emergency Fed facilities

Acharya & Mora (2015): Link to KRS

How this qualifies KRS (2002):

The Synergy is Real but Conditional

When KRS works:

  • Market-wide liquidity shocks
  • Banking sector seen as safe
  • “Flight to safety” → deposits flow in
  • \(\rho < 0\) (negative correlation)

Examples: - 1998 LTCM crisis - Normal CP market stress

When KRS fails:

  • Crisis within banking system
  • Bank solvency questioned
  • Deposits flow out or don’t arrive
  • \(\rho > 0\) (positive correlation)

Example: - 2007-2009 Financial Crisis

Critical Insight

KRS synergy requires banking sector credibility. Without it, deposits and commitments become positively correlated risks rather than a hedge

Survived 2008 only via massive government intervention

Conclusion: The Complete Picture

The Complete Picture

Core Insight from KRS (2002)

Deposits and loan commitments are both forms of liquidity provision

Synergy from sharing buffer stock of liquid assets

Key Empirical Findings:

  1. Banks dominate commitment lending
  2. More deposits → More liquid assets
  3. More deposits → More commitments
  4. Synergy robust to endogeneity concerns

What the Extensions Taught Us

From Gatev & Strahan (2006)

Validation & Enhancement

  • Correlation (\(\rho\)) is actually negative during market crises
  • Banks are “safe havens” → deposits flow in when commitments draw down
  • Buffer dynamically replenishes when most needed
  • Synergy is stronger than KRS initially modeled

From Gatev, Schuermann & Strahan (2009)

Market-Based Evidence

  • Synergy shows up in stock volatility, not just quantities
  • High-deposit banks have lower systematic risk despite commitment exposure
  • Effect is state-dependent: Strongest during liquidity stress

What the Extensions Taught Us (cont.)

From Acharya & Mora (2015)

The Boundary Condition

  • During 2007-2009: Synergy strained and nearly broke
  • When crisis is within banking system, deposits dry up
  • Correlation (\(\rho\)) turns positive → Synergy becomes vulnerability
  • Survived only via massive government intervention

The Refined View

The deposit-commitment synergy is real, powerful, but conditional

Works when banking sector credible; fails when bank solvency questioned

Limitations of This Research

Model Limitations (KRS 2002)

  • Simplified framework: Only 3 dates, binary shocks
  • Static analysis: Doesn’t model dynamic adjustment of buffer stock
  • No strategic behavior: Borrowers and depositors don’t react to bank policy
  • Ignores competition: Market power assumed but not fully modeled

Empirical Limitations

KRS (2002): - Cross-sectional data (1993) → Can’t establish causality definitively - IV approach clever but relies on strong assumptions - Doesn’t directly observe \(\rho\) (the correlation)

Extension papers: - G&S (2006, 2009): Focused on specific crisis episodes (CP market freezes) - A&M (2015): One crisis (2007-09) → Generalizability?

Questions for Future Research

1. What triggers the regime shift?

  • When exactly does \(\rho\) flip from negative to positive?
  • Can we develop early warning indicators?
  • Is it a smooth transition or sudden break?

2. Optimal bank structure?

  • How much commitment capacity per dollar of deposits?
  • Does optimal ratio vary by bank size, type, market conditions?
  • What’s the welfare-maximizing level of synergy?

Questions for Future Research (cont.)

3. International evidence?

  • Does synergy hold in different institutional settings?
  • Role of deposit insurance design (explicit vs. implicit)?
  • Countries without deposit insurance → Does synergy still emerge?

4. Real-time risk management:

  • Can banks track \(\rho\) dynamically using transaction data?
  • How should commitment pricing adjust to correlation risk?

Questions for Future Research (cont.)

5. Global banking complications:

  • How does synergy work for multinational banks?
  • Can foreign deposits hedge domestic commitment risk?

6. COVID-19 as a natural experiment:

  • Did synergy hold during pandemic-induced crisis?
  • PPP loans + deposit inflows → New evidence on \(\rho\)?
  • What was different from 2007-09?

Policy Research Agenda

Unresolved Policy Questions

For Regulators: - How to incorporate correlation risk into stress tests? - Should liquidity coverage ratios be state-dependent? - Optimal capital requirements given synergy benefits and tail risks?

For Central Banks: - How does synergy affect monetary policy transmission? - Design of emergency liquidity facilities to support (not replace) synergy? - Communication strategies to maintain banking sector credibility?

For Macroprudential Policy: - Should commitment exposure limits vary with deposit funding? - How to prevent synchronized correlation shifts across banks? - Role of countercyclical buffers in preserving synergy?

Final Takeaways

1. Economic Efficiency

Banks’ combination of deposits and commitments reflects real synergy, not just historical accident or regulatory artifact

  • Natural hedge during market stress (most of the time)
  • Lower funding costs and systematic risk
  • Efficient provision of liquidity to the economy

2. State-Dependent Performance

The synergy is conditional on market perceptions of banking sector health

  • Normal times: \(\rho < 0\) → Stabilizing force
  • Crisis times: \(\rho > 0\) → Amplifying force
  • Same structure, opposite effects depending on context

Final Takeaways (cont.)

3. Regulatory Challenge

Policy must balance competing objectives:

  • Preserve efficiency gains from synergy in normal times
  • Limit systemic vulnerability when synergy breaks down
  • Maintain credible backstops without excessive moral hazard

Implication: Not a binary choice (narrow banking vs. status quo), but nuanced regulation recognizing both benefits and risks

Questions?
